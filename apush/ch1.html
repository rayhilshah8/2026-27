Comparison

Why did Native societies differ so widely across North America?

click to reveal ↓
Geography and available resources shaped different lifeways. Three-sister farming (corn, beans, squash) supported dense, sedentary populations in the East — enough to build a city like Cahokia. Poor soil regions like the Canadian Shield instead favored small, mobile hunting-and-fishing bands. Complexity ranged from scattered kin-bands to Mississippian mound-building chiefdoms, all before any European contact. SAQ angle: Compare the economic and social organization of two Native American societies before 1607.
Causation

How did humans first reach a continent with no known land bridge to Europe?

click to reveal ↓
During the last Ice Age, lower sea levels exposed a land corridor across the Bering Strait, letting migrating peoples walk from Siberia into present-day Alaska, likely following megafauna herds. Over thousands of years their descendants spread across two continents, eventually diversifying into the whole range of societies covered in this chapter — from small mobile bands to empires like the Aztec and Inca. SAQ angle: Explain how migration patterns before 1491 contributed to the diversity of Native American societies.
Historiography

Did Columbus really "discover" something already known?

click to reveal ↓
Norse voyagers led by Leif Erikson reached North America (a place they called Vinland, likely modern Newfoundland) around 1000 CE — roughly 500 years before Columbus. But that contact was brief, isolated, and left no lasting link between the hemispheres. Columbus's 1492 voyage mattered historically not because it came first, but because it triggered sustained, large-scale exchange between two worlds that had barely known of each other — a good reminder that "discovery" in textbooks usually means "first contact that stuck," not literally first contact. SAQ angle: Explain why the Columbian Exchange, rather than earlier Norse contact, marks the start of sustained interaction between the hemispheres.
Causation

Why did Portugal's voyages along the African coast set the template for later colonization?

click to reveal ↓
Starting in the early 1400s, Portuguese expeditions backed by Prince Henry the Navigator sailed down the West African coast, establishing trading posts for gold, ivory, and enslaved people — decades before Columbus ever sailed. This built a working model that Spain and other powers later transplanted onto the Americas almost unchanged: plantation agriculture, forced labor, and a race-based labor hierarchy. SAQ angle: Explain one way Portuguese activity in Africa influenced the later development of European colonies in the Americas.
Causation

What forces launched the European Age of Exploration?

click to reveal ↓
Rising nation-states — Spain unified after Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile's 1469 marriage — gave monarchs the centralized funding and military power to sponsor voyages. Middlemen controlling overland Asian trade routes made spices and silk expensive, motivating a search for a direct sea route. The caravel made long ocean voyages against the wind possible, and emerging capitalism gave investors a financial incentive to fund risky ventures. SAQ angle: Explain ONE cause of the European Age of Exploration in the late 15th century.
Causation

How did Cortes and Pizarro conquer massive empires with tiny forces?

click to reveal ↓
Disease devastated populations with no immunity to Old World epidemics. Both Hernan Cortes and Francisco Pizarro exploited internal divisions — Cortes allied with rivals of the Aztec, aided by his interpreter Malinche, while Moctezuma's hesitant response gave the Spanish time to maneuver. Superior military technology (steel, horses, gunpowder) sealed the outcome, even after setbacks like the Noche Triste, when Aztec forces temporarily drove Cortes's army out of Tenochtitlan. SAQ angle: Explain ONE reason Spanish conquistadores were able to defeat large Native American empires.
Continuity & Change

What was the Columbian Exchange, and how did it reorder both hemispheres?

click to reveal ↓
The Columbian Exchange moved disease, plants, animals, and people across the Atlantic in both directions. Old World smallpox devastated Native populations with catastrophic death tolls; the Old World also brought horses, cattle, and wheat. In return, the New World gave Europe corn, potatoes, and tobacco, fueling population growth abroad. It's one of the most consequential ecological events in human history — and it started with a single 1492 voyage. SAQ angle: Explain the effects of the Columbian Exchange on ONE region of the world.
Historiography

Was Spanish colonization uniquely brutal, or does the "Black Legend" oversimplify it?

click to reveal ↓
The encomienda system extracted forced labor and tribute from Native peoples under the guise of "protection" and conversion. Abuses documented by the priest Bartolome de Las Casas fed the Black Legend — a narrative, later amplified by rival powers like England, portraying Spanish rule as exceptionally cruel compared to other European empires, even though most colonizers ran exploitative systems of their own. Worth asking whose interests each version of the story served. SAQ angle: Explain how and why historical interpretations of Spanish colonization have differed over time.
Continuity & Change

How did Native peoples resist European control?

click to reveal ↓
Resistance took many forms across two centuries. The Noche Triste (1520) saw Aztec forces briefly expel Cortes. The Battle of Acoma (1599) ended in brutal Spanish suppression of Pueblo resistance in New Mexico. Eight decades later, Pope's Rebellion (1680) succeeded in driving the Spanish out of New Mexico for over a decade — proof that resistance persisted, and sometimes won, even under sustained colonial pressure. SAQ angle: Compare two instances of Native American resistance to European colonization before 1700.
Causation

Why did England, France, and Spain end up competing for the same territory?

click to reveal ↓
Early explorers staked overlapping claims: Giovanni Caboto (John Cabot) sailed for England along the Atlantic coast in 1497; Robert de La Salle claimed the Mississippi corridor for France in 1682; Spain held Mexico, the Southwest, and Florida, later extending into California through Father Junipero Serra's mission system. Three empires drawing rival maps of the same continent set up centuries of conflict over land, resources, and Native alliances. SAQ angle: Explain ONE cause of imperial rivalry among European powers in North America.
Comparison

How did mixed-ancestry populations reshape Spanish colonial society?

click to reveal ↓
Unlike the English colonies, which developed rigid racial separation, Spanish America saw extensive intermarriage between colonizers and Native peoples, producing a mestizo population that became central to colonial social structure. This is an early preview of a contrast the course returns to often: Spanish colonial society blended racial categories in ways English settlements largely did not. SAQ angle: Compare Spanish and English approaches to race and social structure in their American colonies.

Ancient, mineral-rich rock formation covering much of northeastern North America; its thin, poor soil made large-scale farming difficult.

Andean empire centered in Peru, known for an extensive road network and centralized administration; conquered by Francisco Pizarro in 1532–33.

Why it matters: Comparison material for centralized New World empires alongside the Aztecs.

Aztecsclick to flip

Powerful empire centered at Tenochtitlan in central Mexico; conquered by Hernan Cortes in 1519–21.

Why it matters: Central case study for how disease, alliances, and technology enabled conquest.

Nation-statesclick to flip

Centralized states unified under a strong monarch, like Spain after Ferdinand and Isabella's marriage.

Why it matters: Explains why exploration became state-sponsored rather than purely private — a causation building block for the Age of Exploration.

Cahokiaclick to flip

The largest pre-Columbian city north of Mexico (near modern St. Louis), built by Mississippian mound-building culture.

Why it matters: Evidence of complex, urbanized Native societies existing well before European contact.

Three-sister farmingclick to flip

Growing corn, beans, and squash together, a technique that enriched soil and supported dense, settled populations in eastern North America.

Why it matters: Key evidence for comparison questions about Native lifeways and social complexity.

Middlemenclick to flip

Intermediary traders (Italian city-states, Muslim merchants) who controlled land routes into Asia and raised the price of goods reaching Europe.

Why it matters: A direct cause of Europe's push to find a sea route to Asia.

Caravelclick to flip

A small, maneuverable Portuguese ship with lateen sails that could sail effectively against the wind.

Why it matters: The technological breakthrough that made transoceanic exploration physically possible.

Plantationclick to flip

A large agricultural estate, often worked by enslaved or forced labor; first developed on Atlantic islands before spreading to the Americas.

Why it matters: The origin point of the slave-labor economic model later central to American colonization.

Columbian exchangeclick to flip

The transfer of plants, animals, disease, and people between the Old World and New World after 1492.

Why it matters: One of the highest-yield concepts in the whole course — shows up in comparison and CCOT prompts constantly.

1494 agreement dividing the New World between Spain and Portugal along a line, without any Native input.

Encomiendaclick to flip

A Spanish system granting colonists the right to Native labor and tribute in exchange for "protection" and Christianization.

Why it matters: A de facto forced-labor system — useful for comparing labor systems across empires.

Noche tristeclick to flip

The "Sad Night" of 1520, when Aztec forces drove Cortes's army out of Tenochtitlan with heavy Spanish losses.

Capitalismclick to flip

An economic system built around private investment and profit motive, emerging in Europe alongside the Age of Exploration.

Why it matters: Provided the financial mechanism (and later joint-stock companies) that funded colonization.

Mestizosclick to flip

People of mixed European and Native American ancestry, common in Spanish America.

Why it matters: Highlights a key contrast with English colonies, which drew much sharper racial lines.

Conquistadoresclick to flip

Spanish soldier-adventurers who led military expeditions to conquer and claim land in the Americas.

Why it matters: The agents carrying out Spain's empire-building on the ground.

Battle of Acomaclick to flip

1599 conflict in which Spanish forces brutally suppressed a Pueblo revolt at Acoma in present-day New Mexico.

Why it matters: Shows the harsh reprisals Native resistance could provoke on the northern frontier.

Pope's Rebellionclick to flip

1680 Pueblo uprising that successfully drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for over a decade.

Why it matters: A rare example of Native resistance that actually succeeded, at least temporarily.

Black Legendclick to flip

The narrative, fed partly by Las Casas's own writings and later amplified by rival powers, that Spanish colonization was uniquely brutal.

Why it matters: A historiography angle — ask whose interests a historical narrative serves.
